Lecture 4 — Recap of Probability Theory - 1, Part 3

Pushforward measures and distribution functions

Author

Notes based on Prof. Prathosh A.P. (NPTEL / IISc)

Video: Lec 04 — Recap of Probability Theory - 1, Part 3

1. Setup from Lectures 2–3

We have the probability triplet \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) and a random variable

\[ X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \]

(or later \(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{d}\)).

Question of this lecture: what happens to the measure \(P\) when we pass to the range of \(X\)?

2. Which subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) do we use? Borel sets

Not every subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) is treated as an “event” in the range. We use a standard family: the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).

Intuition used in class: building blocks are half-lines of the form

\[ (-\infty,\ x] \]

(and the \(\sigma\)-algebra they generate). In \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\), the analogous building blocks are Cartesian products of such half-lines (rectangles unbounded toward \(-\infty\) in each coordinate).

Random variables are defined so that inverse images of these Borel sets land back in \(\mathcal{F}\) (measurability) — so probabilities remain well-defined.

3. Pushforward measure = distribution of \(X\)

\(X\) maps outcomes in \(\Omega\) to numbers in \(\mathbb{R}\). Subsets of \(\Omega\) map (via inverse images) to subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Because \(P\) lives on events in \(\Omega\), there is an induced measure on (Borel) subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\). That induced measure is called:

Name used in class Same idea
Pushforward measure \(P\) “pushed” through \(X\)
Distribution of \(X\) law of \(X\)
Distribution function / CDF common 1-D encoding of that law

Picture: one measurable space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) + a map \(X\) \(\Rightarrow\) a new measure space on the range side.

Course notation

Script / calligraphic \(P\) with a mark for \(X\) means the distribution of the random variable \(X\). In these notes we write \(P_{X}\).

4. Definition in one dimension (CDF view)

For \(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) and a real number \(x\),

\[ P_{X}(x) \;=\; P\bigl( X^{-1}((-\infty,\ x]) \bigr) \;=\; P\bigl(\{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \le x\}\bigr). \]

In words:

  1. Build the Borel set \((-\infty,\ x]\) in \(\mathbb{R}\).
  2. Take its inverse image under \(X\) (a subset of \(\Omega\)).
  3. Evaluate the original probability measure \(P\) on that event.

That value is the distribution function (CDF) at \(x\).

Endpoint convention: the interval is typically closed at \(x\) and open toward \(-\infty\), i.e. \((-\infty,\ x]\).

Probability is always assigned to a set (an event). Evaluating the distribution at \(x\) is still “probability of a set” — the set \(\{\omega : X(\omega)\le x\}\).

5. How is \(P\) “assigned” in the real world?

Class answer: nature / the labeling process assigns the underlying measure. A radiologist’s intuition is hard to put into equations — we treat it as given by the problem.

Our engineering job (central theme of the course):

Given samples from the range of random variables, estimate the underlying distribution / measure.

That is the probabilistic formulation of ML that the next lectures will connect to function approximation.

6. The working triplet from now on

Pushing forward gives a new triplet on the observation side, schematically

\[ \bigl(\mathbb{R},\ \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\ P_{X}\bigr). \]

Once data are vectors from sensors, we mostly talk and compute in this pushforward world, while remembering that an underlying \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) and map \(X\) sit behind it.

That is why random variables were invented: not decoration — the device that moves us from abstract \(\Omega\) to numbers with a well-defined distribution.

7. Vector-valued random variables

In ML, usually

\[ X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{d}. \]

(“Vector-valued RV” just means: the range consists of vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\).)

Borel building blocks in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)

Cartesian products of half-lines, e.g. in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\):

\[ (-\infty,\ x_1] \times (-\infty,\ x_2]. \]

Geometrically: everything “southwest” of the point \((x_1,x_2)\) in the plane (with the usual closed/open conventions on edges).

Distribution at a vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\)

Input to \(P_{X}\) is an element of the range of \(X\). For vector \(X\),

\[ P_{X}(x) \;=\; P\Bigl( X^{-1}\bigl( (-\infty,x_1]\times\cdots\times(-\infty,x_d] \bigr) \Bigr). \]

Again: inverse image under \(X\), then apply \(P\).

Notation clash warning (from class): capital \(X\) for the RV vs small/bold \(x\) for a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) — same letter family, different objects.

8. Distribution vs density (preview)

  • The distribution function / pushforward is a valid probability measure (on Borel sets).
  • A density (when it exists) is not itself a probability: evaluating a density at a single point does not give \(P(X=x)\) in continuous models.

Formal density discussion continues later; do not confuse “PDF value at a point” with “probability of an event.”

9. Multiple random variables (setup for next class)

There can be several random variables (hence possibly several experiments / sample spaces, or one joint experiment — your modeling choice).

X-ray + disease (two views)

View Meaning
One experiment “Get disease and get imaged” as a single process \(\to\) one \(\Omega\), maybe vector-valued \(X\)
Two experiments Disease outcome vs imaging outcome as separate RVs that may be correlated

Both are allowed; random experiments are chosen by the modeler.

Vector RV vs many scalars

A Gaussian vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) can be seen as:

  • one RV with range \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\), or
  • \(d\) correlated scalar RVs (covariance / correlation matrix).

Same mathematics, two stories.

Discriminative vs generative (preview)

In this worldview they can look unified: the label is simply another coordinate of a vector-valued random variable; conditioning corresponds to restricting to appropriate events (set operations on the underlying spaces).

Next lectures: joint / conditional distributions more carefully, then ML as learning distributions (linked back to function approximation).

10. What to refresh before next class

  • Joint distributions of several RVs
  • Conditional distributions (ask: which events am I conditioning on?)
  • Discrete vs continuous RVs
  • Always relate formulas back to sets in \(\Omega\) and inverse images under \(X\)

  5. Who “assigns” \(P\) in applications, and what do ML engineers estimate?
  6. Why is evaluating a continuous density at a point not a probability?
