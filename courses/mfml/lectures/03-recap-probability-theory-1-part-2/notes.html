Lecture 3 — Recap of Probability Theory - 1, Part 2

Probability triplet and random variables

Author

Notes based on Prof. Prathosh A.P. (NPTEL / IISc)

Video: Lec 03 — Recap of Probability Theory - 1, Part 2

1. Where we left off

Lecture 2 gave us:

  • Sample space \(\Omega\)
  • Events \(\mathcal{F}\) (suitable subsets of \(\Omega\))
  • Probability measure \(P : \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]\)

Question for ML: where does this fit, and what do we do next?

2. The probability triplet

The package

\[ (\Omega,\ \mathcal{F},\ P) \]

is the probability triplet (probability space).

Useful analogy

Abstract probability Concrete geometry on the line
\(\Omega\) \(\mathbb{R}\)
events \(\mathcal{F}\) (nice) subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\)
\(P\) length (Lebesgue) measure

Same pattern: a ground set, a family of subsets, a measure on those subsets.

3. Why we cannot stop at the triplet

\(\Omega\) is often abstract and non-numeric (heads/tails; “someone got imaged and labeled”).

Engineers need numbers — we add, subtract, run algorithms, store arrays.

Slogan from class: think abstract, act concrete.

  • Thinking: probability space / experiment
  • Acting: concrete observations (vectors on a computer)

So we need a bridge from abstraction \(\to\) something we can compute with.

Sensors / observations (X-ray story)

Part of \(\Omega\): the whole story (nodule develops, person visits, imaging happens, …).

What we actually store: an X-ray image \(\to\) a vector on disk.

We work with vectors; we started from an abstract sample space. What connects them?

4. Random variable = the bridge

A random variable is a function

\[ X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \]

(or, more useful for ML, \(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{d}\)).

It maps each abstract outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\) to a number (or vector) we can manipulate.

Why we need it

Without \(X\) With \(X\)
Only abstract outcomes (H/T, “process happened”) Concrete numbers / vectors
Hard to do algebra / ML Can compute, fit models, store data

You cannot stop at \(\Omega\) if you want engineering work. The extra map \(X\) is not optional decoration — it is why RVs were defined.

Famous misnomer

A random variable is neither random nor a variable.
It is a deterministic function \(\Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) (or \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)).

(Name is historical; treat it as a function.)

Range need not be all of \(\mathbb{R}\)

  • Can be integers, a finite set, etc.
  • In practice for images / features: range is \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)
  • Lecture generalizes: think \(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{d}\)

5. What is a data point? (two layers of truth)

Deterministic language (Lecture 1)

An image is a point / vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\).

Probabilistic language (this lecture)

Still a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\), but that \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the range of a random variable \(X\) defined on an underlying sample space \(\Omega\) from a random experiment we do not fully observe.

Mental checklist whenever you see a datapoint \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\):

random experiment
      |
      v
sample space Omega
      |
      v
random variable X : Omega -> R^d
      |
      v
observed vector x  (element of the range of X)

Folder of images = many such range elements (realizations / observations of \(X\)).

6. Preview: what happens to \(P\)? (next ideas)

Defining \(X\) raises the natural question:

We had \(P\) on subsets of \(\Omega\). Under \(X\), what becomes of that measure on subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)?

Answer (named in class, developed next): pushforward measure, which becomes the distribution of \(X\).

Also mentioned: the image of the event \(\sigma\)-algebra under \(X\) relates to the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) (measure-theoretic detail — intuition: “nice” subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)).

This lecture stopped as that transition began; later parts fill in distribution / density.

Exercise left in class

Is a random variable necessarily bijective? (Think carefully; not required for this course’s main path.)

7. One-page summary

Object Role
\((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) Probability triplet — abstract uncertainty
Sensor / measurement How we observe the world
\(X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\) Random variable — bridge to numbers
Datapoint \(x\) Element of the range of \(X\)

ML connection: classical “vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)” sit inside a probabilistic story: experiment \(\to\) \(\Omega\) \(\to\) \(X\) \(\to\) data.

8. Self-check

  1. Write down the probability triplet and one analogy with length on \(\mathbb{R}\).
  2. Why is \(\Omega\) alone not enough for ML engineering?
  3. Define a random variable formally (domain and codomain).
  4. Why is “random variable” a misnomer?
  5. In the X-ray example, what is abstract (\(\Omega\)) vs what is concrete (range of \(X\))?
  6. Rephrase “an image is a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\)” in probabilistic language.
  7. What question about \(P\) does introducing \(X\) force us to ask next?
